# Our Sun

Our Sun is a 4.5 billion-year-old yellow dwarf star, a hot glowing ball of hydrogen and helium at the center of our solar system. It is about 93 million miles from Earth, and it is our solar system's only star. Without the Sun's energy, life as we know it could not exist on our home planet.

## A Medium-Sized Star

The Sun is the largest object in our solar system. Its diameter is about 865,000 miles, roughly 100 times wider than Earth. It would take more than 330,000 Earths to match the mass of the Sun, and 1.3 million Earths to fill its volume.

Yet the Sun is only an average star. Stars up to 100 times larger have been found, and many solar systems have more than one star. By studying our Sun, scientists can better understand the workings of distant stars.

## How the Sun Formed and How It Will End

The Sun formed about 4.6 billion years ago in a giant, spinning cloud of gas and dust called the solar nebula. As the nebula collapsed under its own gravity, it spun faster and flattened into a disk. Most of its material was pulled toward the center to form the Sun, which accounts for 99.8% of our solar system's mass. Much of the rest formed the planets.

Like all stars, the Sun will eventually run out of energy. When it starts to die, it will expand into a red giant, becoming so large that it will engulf Mercury and Venus, and possibly Earth. Scientists predict the Sun is a little less than halfway through its lifetime and will last another 5 billion years before it becomes a white dwarf.

## Inside the Sun

The Sun has several regions. Its interior includes the core, the radiative zone, and the convection zone. Moving outward come the visible surface, called the photosphere, then the chromosphere, the transition zone, and finally the corona, the Sun's expansive outer atmosphere.

The core is the hottest part of the Sun. Nuclear reactions there fuse hydrogen into helium and power the Sun's heat and light. Temperatures top 27 million degrees Fahrenheit, and the core is about 8 times as dense as gold.

Energy from the core is carried outward by radiation, which bounces around the radiative zone for about 170,000 years before reaching the convection zone. There, large bubbles of hot plasma rise toward the photosphere.

## The Surface and the Atmosphere

The Sun does not have a solid surface. The part we call its surface is the photosphere, which means "light sphere." It emits the most visible light, and at about 10,000 degrees Fahrenheit it is much cooler than the core.

Above it lie the chromosphere and the corona. In one of the Sun's biggest mysteries, the corona gets hotter the farther it stretches from the surface, reaching up to 3.5 million degrees Fahrenheit. Imagine walking away from a bonfire only to get warmer. The source of this heating is a major unsolved puzzle.

## Sunspots, Flares, and Eruptions

Sunspots look like dark holes, but they are areas slightly cooler than the surrounding surface, created where the Sun's magnetic field pokes out from the interior. Solar flares are bursts of light and particles triggered by the release of magnetic energy; they are the most powerful explosions in the solar system. Coronal mass ejections are immense clouds of magnetized particles blasted into space at over a million miles per hour, often following a flare.

## The Solar Cycle and Space Weather

The Sun goes through phases of high and low activity called the solar cycle. About every 11 years, its north and south magnetic poles swap. At the height of the cycle, sunspots, flares, and coronal mass ejections are common.

Solar activity can affect us on Earth. Space weather can interfere with satellites, GPS, and radio, and it can cripple power grids. In 1859, the Carrington Event sent telegraph systems haywire and lit skies around the world with auroras. In 1989, a solar storm plunged 6 million people in Quebec into darkness for 9 hours.
