# Apollo 11: The First Crewed Moon Landing

The primary objective of Apollo 11 was to complete a national goal set by President John F. Kennedy on May 25, 1961: perform a crewed lunar landing and return to Earth. Additional objectives included scientific exploration by the lunar module crew, a television camera to transmit signals to Earth, and a set of experiments left on the surface.

## The Crew and the Spacecraft

Apollo 11 carried three astronauts. Neil Armstrong was the commander, Edwin "Buzz" Aldrin was the lunar module pilot, and Michael Collins was the command module pilot. The command and service module was named Columbia, and the lunar module was named Eagle.

## Launch and the Journey to the Moon

Apollo 11 launched from Cape Kennedy on July 16, 1969, into an initial Earth orbit of 114 by 116 miles. Two hours and 44 minutes after launch, the third stage of the Saturn V reignited for a burn of nearly six minutes, placing Apollo 11 on its way to the moon.

Columbia then separated from the stage, turned around, and docked with Eagle. The first color television transmission to Earth came during the coast toward the moon. The launch had been so accurate that only one of the four planned midcourse corrections was needed.

On July 18, Armstrong and Aldrin climbed through the docking tunnel from Columbia to Eagle to check out the lunar module and make the second television broadcast.

## Into Lunar Orbit

On July 19, after Apollo 11 had flown behind the moon out of contact with Earth, the main engine fired for almost six minutes to place the spacecraft into an elliptical lunar orbit of 69 by 190 miles. A second, shorter burn tightened it to 62 by 70 miles.

## Landing in the Sea of Tranquility

On July 20, Armstrong and Aldrin entered Eagle, made a final check, and undocked from Columbia. The descent engine fired to begin the approach. Partially piloted by hand, Eagle landed in the Sea of Tranquility, about four miles downrange from the predicted point and almost a minute and a half early. The powered descent ran 40 seconds longer than planned because Armstrong steered to avoid a crater during the final phase.

## The First Steps

The flight plan called for a four-hour rest before the moonwalk, but it was advanced to begin as soon as possible. About 109 hours and 42 minutes after launch, Armstrong stepped onto the moon, describing it as "one small step for a man, one giant leap for mankind." An estimated 650 million people watched. About 20 minutes later, Aldrin followed him.

The two astronauts ranged up to 300 feet from Eagle. Aldrin deployed a package of early scientific experiments, and both gathered and described samples of the lunar surface. They left behind medallions honoring astronauts and cosmonauts who had died, and a small silicon disk carrying goodwill messages from 73 countries. The moonwalk lasted more than two and a half hours.

## Leaving the Moon

Armstrong and Aldrin spent 21 hours and 36 minutes on the moon's surface. After a rest that included seven hours of sleep, Eagle's ascent engine fired and lifted them into lunar orbit, where they rejoined Collins in Columbia.

On July 21, Columbia's main engine fired for two and a half minutes behind the moon to send the crew home. Only one small midcourse correction was needed on the return.

## Splashdown

Re-entry began on July 24. The service module separated, and the command module turned its heat shield forward. After a flight of 195 hours, 18 minutes, and 35 seconds, Apollo 11 splashed down in the Pacific Ocean, 13 miles from the recovery ship USS Hornet. Bad weather had moved the landing point about 250 miles. Over eight days the mission had traveled 953,054 miles.
