# How Vaccines Work

Vaccines work by imitating an infection to engage the body's natural defenses. They help the body learn how to defend itself from disease without the dangers of a full-blown infection. The immune response to a vaccine might cause tiredness and discomfort for a day or two, but the resulting protection can last a lifetime.

## Getting Vaccinated Is Safer Than Getting Sick

Infections are unpredictable and can have long-term consequences. Even mild or symptom-less infections can be deadly. For example, most people infected with the human papillomavirus never show any sign of infection. But for some, the sign appears years later as an aggressive, life-threatening cancer. By then, it's too late to get vaccinated.

## Vaccines Imitate an Infection

An infection is the presence of a disease-causing organism in the body. Vaccines imitate one to engage the body's natural defenses. The active ingredient in every vaccine is an antigen: any substance that causes the immune system to begin producing antibodies. In a vaccine, the antigen could be weakened or killed bacteria or viruses, bits of their exterior surface or genetic material, or a bacterial toxin treated to make it non-toxic.

## How the Body Fights an Infection

Antibodies are proteins produced by white blood cells to identify and neutralize foreign substances. White blood cells are created in the bone marrow and dispersed throughout the body in low numbers, ready to begin multiplying and attacking microbes that are not native to the body.

First, the white blood cells recognize the invader. Then they multiply and attack it. After they have eliminated the infection, they stop multiplying, and their numbers dwindle until only a few are left to keep watch. At that point, a person is considered immunized.

Because immunity can take weeks to develop after vaccination, it is possible to become infected in the weeks immediately following a shot. Even after that, vaccinated people sometimes do get infected. But a vaccinated person is far less likely to die or become seriously ill than someone whose immune system is unprepared.

## Many Vaccines Require More Than One Dose

A single dose of vaccine provides only partial protection. The number of doses needed depends on whether the antigen in the vaccine is alive or not.

Live-attenuated vaccines contain living bacteria or viruses. They offer long-lasting, even lifetime protection with only two doses, but they could cause a life-threatening infection in someone with a weak or suppressed immune system. The chickenpox vaccine and the combined measles, mumps, and rubella vaccine are examples.

Non-live vaccines are safer for people with weak immune systems, but their protection fades over time. They typically require three or more doses, followed by boosters. Infants receive the DTaP vaccine at 2, 4, 6, and 18 months of age, children get boosters when they enter school and middle school, and adults should get a tetanus booster once every 10 years.

Some vaccines must be updated to keep up with mutation-prone viruses. The seasonal flu vaccine is reformulated each year to target the four strains expected to be most common and most dangerous.

## Active and Passive Immunity

To be immune is to be partially or fully resistant to a specific disease. Immunization is the process of becoming resistant, usually by means of a vaccine. Immunity itself comes in two kinds.

Active immunity comes from being exposed to a disease-causing organism, either through infection or through vaccination with killed or weakened bacteria or viruses. It takes longer to develop, but it lasts longer.

Passive immunity is provided by antibodies produced by another person or animal. Full-term babies acquire it from their mother's antibodies during the final months of pregnancy. Its protection is immediate, but it fades within weeks or months.

## Everyone Should Get Recommended Vaccines on Schedule

History shows that vaccines are the safest, most effective way to protect yourself and your family from many preventable diseases. Children and adolescents should get catch-up doses of any missed vaccines as soon as they can. All adults should get tetanus boosters, seasonal flu vaccines, and any vaccines missed in childhood.
